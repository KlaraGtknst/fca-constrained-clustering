Ontologies provide formal and explicit specifications of concepts and their relationships within a domain~\cite{studer:98}.
They support knowledge retrieval, reuse, and rule formulation~\cite{cimiano_learning_2005,yang2012personalized}, and communication through shared conceptualizations~\cite{studer:98,storey:98}. % communication even if different internal knowledge representation, bc conceptualization (ontology) is shared
% In linguistic and lexical-semantic ontologies, they organize hyponym relationships under a common top node~\cite{le2019inferring} and structure information into categories, thereby supporting rule formulation as well as the retrieval from and reuse of knowledge bases~\cite{cimiano_learning_2005,yang2012personalized}.
While manual ontology construction is often regarded as the gold standard, it is costly and typically reflects only a snapshot of evolving knowledge~\cite{li2018unsupervised}.
% solution: automatic concept hierarchy generation
Automatic concept hierarchy generation therefore aims to reduce time and cost~\cite{Aznag2014LeveragingFC,risch2020hierarchical} and extends coverage of domains not represented in existing ontologies.

Existing automatic approaches operate on both structured and unstructured data sources.
% 1. already structured data sources
Structured linguistic resources, such as the semantic dictionary Roget's Thesaurus~\cite{Priss:2004,old:04,Priss:96}, lexical databases such as WordNet~\cite{Priss:96,priss:98,GalnPez2014DiscoveringNS} and FrameNet~\cite{val:05}, and morphological feature systems~\cite{sporleder:02,petersen:04}, can be represented as formal contexts.
% 2. unstructured data sources, e.g., hearst1992hyponyms with Hearst patterns: automatically extract hyponym–hypernym relations; cimiano_learning_2005 with a multi-step NLP pipeline: parser/POS, lemmatizer ...
For unstructured text corpora, formal contexts must first be derived from textual information using methods ranging from pattern-based extraction~\cite{hearst1992hyponyms,le2019inferring} to multi-step \acl{nlp} pipelines that construct binary object-attribute contexts~\cite{cimiano_learning_2005}, as well as approaches using phrases directly as attributes~\cite{Elzinga:09,cigarran_twitter_fca_2016}.


\ac{fca}-based concept hierarchy generation can be viewed as a form of conceptual clustering~\cite{Cimiano2004ComparingCD} that yields intensional descriptions of abstract concepts through their intents~\cite{cimiano_learning_2005}. 
Since each concept is characterized by an explicit set of shared attributes, the resulting hierarchy provides transparent, attribute-based explanations of the relationships between concepts, making \ac{fca}-based hierarchies inherently interpretable compared to similarity-based clustering methods.

Despite their scalability, automatically generated hierarchies may conflict with established expert ontologies, potentially reducing trust in either source.
Ensuring consistency with hierarchical background knowledge therefore requires mechanisms that guide and constrain the generation process.
Existing approaches (Section~\ref{sec:related_work}) include human-in-the-loop strategies~\cite{yang2012personalized}, the use of \acp{llm}~\cite{kommineni2024from,zhang2025teleclass}, constrained \ac{fca}~\cite{belohlavek2011selecting,belohlavek2009formal,belohlavek2006formal,hidayat_constrained_fca_2024,mao_constrained_fca_2017}, and hierarchical constraints in similarity-based settings~\cite{bade2006personalized,bade_hierarchical_2014}. 

% Besides presentation purposes, \ac{fca} has been employed to identify potentially missing thesaurus entries in a specific expert ontology~\cite{zheng2020lexical,hao2021leveraging}. 

To date, the systematic integration of hierarchical background knowledge into \ac{fca}-based concept hierarchy generation through \ac{mlb} constraints has received limited attention.
We address this gap by incorporating \ac{mlb} constraints into the \ac{fca} framework (Section~\ref{sec:method}),\footnote{The code is available at \url{github.com/KlaraGtknst/fca-constrained-clustering}} thereby extending prior work that incorporates pairwise constraints~\cite{belohlavek2011selecting}.
In addition, we present a case study (Section~\ref{sec:case_study}) comparing concept lattices derived from expert-defined \ac{mlb} constraints and from a data-driven topic model through \textit{consistency} and \textit{coherence} lattice intersections at different coherence levels.
